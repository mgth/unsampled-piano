Everything a piano note becomes starts with a few milliseconds of contact: a felt-covered
hammer, thrown by the key, hits the strings from below, squeezes against them and bounces off.
How long it stays, how hard it pushes and how that push rises and falls decide which partials
the string receives — the **timbre**, and how the timbre changes with how hard you play.

## Felt is a nonlinear spring

The model follows Stulov's measured hammers: the felt pushes back with

<p class="formula">F = Q0 · [ u<sup>p</sup> + α · d(u<sup>p</sup>)/dt ]</p>

where *u* is how much it is squeezed (in mm). With *p* around 4, doubling the compression
multiplies the force by sixteen: a hammer thrown twice as fast does not only push twice as
hard, its push gets **shorter and sharper**, and that is what brightens a forte. The second
term is the hysteresis α — felt keeps part of the energy instead of springing back — which
the contact solver applies in a form that can only remove energy, so the strike is stable
whatever the settings.

Real hammers are **layered**: soft felt outside, much denser felt underneath. The model adds
that core below a top-felt depth *d*:

`hardness` is the **voicing** knob a technician has with needles and lacquer: it scales the
top felt's stiffness on every key. The slopes spread it across the keyboard — treble hammers
are smaller and harder — on top of Stulov's own growth of Q0 with the key:

## Weight and strike point

A **heavier** hammer (`hammer_mass_scale`) stays longer on the strings at the same speed:
more energy, a fuller and rounder tone. Light hammers — fortepianos, small uprights — bounce
off sooner, for a thinner, quicker attack.

**Where** it hits matters as much as how. A string struck at a point cannot start the partials
that have a still point (a node) there:

<StrikePoint />

Tuning `strike_ratio` toward the end of the string brightens and thins the tone; further in,
it gets rounder. `contact_width_scale` widens the felt patch touching the string, a gentle
low-pass on the highest partials.
