A piano string is a steel wire, and steel is stiff. A perfectly flexible string would vibrate
at exact multiples of its fundamental; a stiff one resists bending a little more for each
shorter wave, so every partial comes out **sharp**, and more so the higher it is. That is the
piano's **inharmonicity** — part of its slightly metallic colour, and the reason it cannot be
tuned by the textbook.

## Inharmonicity

Each key gets a coefficient B, and partial *k* lands at

<p class="formula">f<sub>k</sub> = k · f0 · √(1 + B · k²)</p>

<Partials />

B is large for the short, thick strings of the treble, and rises again in the bass where the
strings are foreshortened to fit the case. The model uses the two-exponential law of Rigaud,
David and Daudet (2013), fitted on the recordings of a Yamaha C5 (the Salamander Grand):

<CurveChart title="Inharmonicity B" caption="B per key (log scale): the two exponentials meet in the tenor. Fitted on Salamander, rms 0.07 in log B." />

## Stretch

Because the partials are sharp, an octave tuned exactly 2:1 between fundamentals sounds flat:
the upper note's fundamental beats against the lower note's sharp second partial. Tuners
**stretch** the octaves instead — treble tuned sharp, bass flat — until they sound pure. The
model follows a fitted stretch law, in cents from equal temperament:

<p class="formula">cents(n) = a_t · e<sup>k_t (n − 69)</sup> − a_b · e<sup>−k_b (n − 69)</sup> + c</p>

<CurveChart title="Stretch" caption="The default stretch, fitted on Salamander (C8 excluded, rms 2 cents): −16 cents at A0, +25 at C8. Hover to read any key." />

`a4` moves everything: 440 Hz is the modern standard, orchestras often tune to 442, baroque
ensembles to 415 — the *Baroque 415* character preset does exactly that. Changing it rebuilds
the instrument, because each string's mass is computed from its pitch, length and tension.
