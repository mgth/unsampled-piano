# Building the real-time physical piano on a modal core

The state of the art points to one architecture rather than another: **the only complete published piano that runs in real time with longitudinal nonlinearity, double decay and sympathetic resonance is a modal synthesiser**, the one by Bank, Zambon and Fontana (2010). It runs about **10,000 second-order resonators and four 20,000-point convolutions at about 30% of a 2.4 GHz Core 2 Duo** ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). The commercial modelled piano, the reference, also relies on a modal representation. According to its patent, it computes the coupled string–soundboard modes offline using finite elements and a metamodel, then performs real-time additive synthesis of damped partials (the vendor's patent). Since 2021, explicit schemes based on energy quadratisation (IEQ/SAV) integrate the nonlinear hammer–string contact **without iteration, at fixed cost and with proven stability** ([Ducceschi et al. 2021](https://vbn.aau.dk/ws/files/475165562/DucceschiEtal2021JASALinearlyImplicitSchemes.pdf); [van Walstijn et al. 2023](https://www.dafx.de/paper-archive/2023/DAFx23_paper_18.pdf)). Explicit SAV finite-difference strings now run at 1–4% of an Apple M3 core per string, but only on isolated strings, without hammer or bridge ([Risse et al. 2025](https://dafx25.dii.univpm.it/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/DAFx25_paper_24.pdf)). The recommendation is therefore as follows: a Bank-style modal core, explicit quadratised hammer contact, secondary strings coupled one-way to begin with, and a shared modal soundboard in which each microphone is a set of residues. SAV finite differences remain a research track and the waveguide stays out of the core. This core would need on the order of **1 to 2 modern CPU cores** (inference to be validated in Phase 0). The real risk is not computation but data: there is no public inharmonicity table across 88 notes, bridge admittance is only published as curves, and there is neither a contact law for damper felt nor any measurement of keyboard noises. These mechanical noises will therefore remain mimetic. The vendor's patent expires around **the end of 2027** (Google estimate), which sets a natural horizon for release. The Rust ecosystem (nice-plug/clack, cpal 0.18, stable SIMD via pulp or fearless_simd, RealtimeSanitizer) is sufficient, with a licensing caveat on the VST3 bindings, which are under GPLv3.

> Conventions. **[RT proven]**: a real-time implementation has been published. **[offline]**: non-real-time reference simulation. **[inference]**: calculation or reasoning from this report, not measured in a source. The legal points are **not legal advice**.

## Only modal synthesis and waveguides have sustained polyphonic real time

Bank and Chabassier describe the canonical chain: one goes from physics to an accurate numerical model, then to an optimised signal-processing model that runs in real time. Accurate models serve physicists and piano makers; efficient models serve musicians ([Bank & Chabassier, IEEE SPM 2019](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/bank-chabassier-modelbaseddigitalpianosfromphysicstosoundsynthesis-2019)). Historically, there is Garnett's waveguide piano (1987), Smith and Van Duyne's commuted synthesis (1995), the first complete real-time piano by Borin et al. (1997), then the commercial modelled piano (2006) and the Roland V-Piano (2009), the first digital piano based on physical modelling ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)).

The **digital waveguide (DWG)** is the cheapest option: a delay line and a low-order IIR filter in a loop. Most historical piano models adopted it “for its efficiency”. Its weakness is structural. When dispersion is lumped into a single filter, the mode shapes are no longer orthogonal, which prevents accurate modelling of the longitudinal nonlinearity without distributing the filter, and therefore without the cost exploding ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). **Commuted synthesis** is cheaper still, but it assumes a linear time-invariant (LTI) system: it correctly handles neither the re-striking of a string that is still sounding nor the nonlinearity ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf); [Four Decades of Digital Waveguides, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.12878)).

**Modal synthesis** decomposes each string into independent resonators. For a string with hinged ends, each mode obeys ÿ_k + a1,k ẏ_k + a0,k y_k = b0,k F_k, with f_k ≈ f0·k·√(1 + B k²). It is discretised by impulse invariance. The leading zero in the force→displacement response removes the delay-free loop with the hammer, and with it the y[n] ≈ y[n−1] workaround, which becomes unstable at high striking velocities. The force at the bridge is a simple weighted sum of the modal amplitudes ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). The cost is **3 multiplications per sample per excited resonator, 2 in free oscillation**, and SSE vectorisation brought a gain close to ×4. The model offers several advantages: frequency and damping adjustable per partial, easy nonlinear couplings, and avoidable aliasing since the frequencies are known. The authors acknowledge a cost “probably higher” than that of a DWG without nonlinearity ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). Renault et al. state that the commercial piano “is based on modal synthesis” ([Renault, Mignot, Roebel, DAFx 2022](https://dafx.de/paper-archive/2022/papers/DAFx20in22_paper_48.pdf)).

**Energy-conserving finite differences and finite elements** are the physical reference, but remain offline. The Chabassier–Chaigne–Joly–Duruflé model of a Steinway D includes prestressed nonlinear strings, a hysteretic hammer, an orthotropic Reissner–Mindlin soundboard with ribs, and 3D air with PML. It requires **“24 h on 300 processors for one second of sound”**, with Δt = 1 µs and more than 90 million degrees of freedom for the air ([Chabassier, Duruflé, Joly, M2AN Part 2](https://www.numdam.org/item/10.1051/m2an/2015007.pdf)). That amounts to about 2.6×10⁷ core·seconds per second of audio [inference]. In 2004, an FD string with longitudinal motion required fs ≈ 500 kHz, hence monophony. Bank and Sujbert's reduced model (transverse FD plus longitudinal resonators) cost 10 to 20 times as much as a DWG string and allowed 5 to 10 voices ([Bank & Sujbert, DAFx-04](https://dafx.de/paper-archive/2004/P_089.PDF)). OpenPiano, an open-source finite-difference JUCE plugin, is still in alpha with one string per note ([OpenPiano](https://github.com/michele-perrone/OpenPiano)).

The recent breakthrough comes from **energy quadratisation**. Ducceschi and Bilbao first obtained a linearly implicit conservative scheme for the geometrically exact string. It requires a sparse linear solve per step and runs about three times faster than a 10-iteration Newton method, but remains offline in Matlab at 480 kHz ([ICA 2019](https://www.pub.dega-akustik.de/ICA2019/data/articles/001028.pdf); [JSV 2022](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/simulation-of-the-geometrically-exact-nonlinear-string-via-energy/)). Fully explicit SAV variants (interleaved variables, Sherman–Morrison) remove any linear solve. However, they suffer from **drift of the auxiliary variable** (relative error up to 10⁴, audible pitch glides after repeated strikes), which Risse, Hélie and Bilbao correct with a power-balance control term (λ0 ≈ 1000 s⁻¹) at an extra cost of about 40% ([Risse et al., DAFx 2025](https://dafx25.dii.univpm.it/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/DAFx25_paper_24.pdf)). On the modal side, van Walstijn, Bhanuprakash and Chatziioannou build an explicit hammer–string scheme by quadratising the contact energy. It is non-adhesive, allows all parameters to be changed online and holds an **RTF < 0.75 in Matlab on an i7-6700 up to 1000 modes**, whereas the note A0 needs only about 300 ([DAFx 2023](https://www.dafx.de/paper-archive/2023/DAFx23_paper_18.pdf)). The convergence of SAV for nonlinear stiff strings has now been analysed ([Russo, Ducceschi, Bilbao, Nonlinear Dyn. 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11071-026-12708-0)).

The table reveals a clear gap: **no 2024–2026 paper presents a complete real-time piano, including coupled unisons and bridge, beyond Bank 2010**. SAV schemes have only been demonstrated on isolated strings excited by a synthetic force ([Risse et al. 2025](https://dafx25.dii.univpm.it/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/DAFx25_paper_24.pdf)). A Rust project that integrates these building blocks is therefore doing research, not just engineering.

## The commercial piano computes its modes offline and synthesises damped partials

Its founder, an applied mathematician, set up the vendor in 2006 with academic backing. He describes his technique as “modal analysis” combined with academic parametrisation techniques. In 2010, the team acknowledged that the commercial modelled piano “needs quite a lot of CPU”, with more than 80% load on a netbook (CDM interview, 2010). **The vendor's patent** (priority 2006, filed 2007, granted 2011, estimated expiry **end of 2027**) describes the architecture. Each string is an elastic beam (inharmonicity) with a “quadratic effect”. The soundboard is modelled with finite elements (shell, laminate with fibre orientation and ribs, or 3D). Strings and soundboard are solved as **a single coupled system**, including sympathetic resonance and unison detuning, through a nonlinear complex eigenvalue problem. The signal is s(t) = Σ a_n e^(−d_n t) sin(2π f_n t + θ_n) + b(t), where b is a percussive noise. f_n and d_n come from the eigenvalues. a_n and θ_n come from **excitation signals recorded or computed by a hammer/string model, stored per striking intensity**. A non-real-time “presynthesis” module goes from physical parameters to modal data via a **metamodel** built on a design of experiments (Latin hypercube, sparse grids; kriging, RBF, neural networks, Padé approximants…) (the vendor's patent).

This reading agrees with what can be observed of the product. The commercial piano's v4 manual states that bass notes require more computation, which is consistent with a cost proportional to the number of partials below Nyquist. It also specifies that polyphony counts “notes *and* sympathetic resonances”, that the per-partial spectral profile and the equaliser act “before the sound is computed”, and that the internal sampling rate goes up to 192 kHz in the Pro edition (vendor's manual, v4). The installation takes about 54 MB for v9 (Sound On Sound review of v9). Wikipedia states that pedal, key-release and hammer noises are **sampled**, but the article carries “primary sources” banners and no confirmation from the vendor was found (Wikipedia). If this is correct, this project's “zero samples” constraint is **stricter than the commercial piano itself** as far as noises are concerned.

The exposed parameters provide a functional-parity checklist. The v4 manual describes tuning (reference pitch, temperament that accounts for inharmonicity, unison width, octave stretching, “direct sound duration”, i.e. double decay), voicing (hammer hardness at three velocities, per-partial spectral profile, strike point), design (soundboard impedance, cutoff, Q factor, string length up to “10 m”, sympathetic resonance, duplex), action (damper position and efficiency, last damper, noises), pedals (progressive, sostenuto, harmonic, una corda), as well as microphones, lid, speed of sound and binaural head (vendor's manual, v4). v8 (16 November 2022) added sympathetic resonance driven by an audio input and user-defined stretching points (Gearspace). v9 (9.0.1 in October 2025) brought an improved soundboard, a hammer tone setting, a “thunder” effect pedal and up to 8 microphones placed in 3D with directivity (Sound On Sound review of v9). According to a forum user, the commercial piano did not support MIDI 2.0 in 2024, only 14-bit velocity via CC#88 (vendor's forum). This is an unofficial claim.

Few competing products are genuinely modelled. The **Roland V-Piano** (2009) processes the three strings of a note separately, isolates and resynthesises the frame “thump”, and adds a virtual soundboard ([Roland Engineering](https://articles.roland.com/roland-engineering-v-piano/)). **Viscount Physis** is a physical model developed by three Italian universities. Its patent WO2013135627A1 cites the vendor's patent ([MMR](https://mmrmagazine.com/physical-modeling-and-the-physis-piano/); Google Patents). **Arturia Piano V** exposes string age, unison detuning and stretching, with no technical publication ([Arturia](https://www.arturia.com/products/software-instruments/piano-v/overview)). Yamaha VRM and Roland SuperNATURAL start from **samples and add modelled resonance and damping** ([Yamaha FAQ](https://faq.yamaha.com/usa/s/article/000001306); [Roland](https://articles.roland.com/roland-engineering-v-piano/)). The mass market thus confirms that the added value of physics is concentrated where sampling fails: pedal, sympathy, partial damping. Open sources reusable as references include **faust-coopiano** (hammers, unison strings, modal soundboard; MIT) ([GitHub](https://github.com/sletz/faust-coopiano)) and the STK/Faust piano, commuted below E6 and modal above ([faust-stk](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~rmichon/publications/doc/DAFx11-Faust-STK.pdf)). Finally, the commercial piano serves as a **reference renderer** in MIR research: DDSP-Piano remains below it in MOS (52 listeners) ([Renault et al. 2022](https://dafx.de/paper-archive/2022/papers/DAFx20in22_paper_48.pdf)), and several MUSHRA tests from 2022–2025 use it as a condition ([arXiv 2508.07751](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07751); [arXiv 2501.10222](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.10222)). **No independent perceptual study compares the commercial piano with sampled pianos or with recordings.**

### The vendor's patent expires at the end of 2027 (not legal advice)

The patent is active, with an expiry estimated by Google at **the end of 2027**. This date is a few months beyond 20 years from the 2007 filing, which suggests a term adjustment [inference]. The family includes European, Japanese and Chinese patents (Google Patents). The non-US expiry dates have not been checked. They should fall around 20 years after their respective filings if the renewal fees have been paid [inference, to be checked]. The claims themselves were not analysed word for word in the research notes. Only the description is known.

Technically, the architecture recommended here departs from the patent's description on three verifiable points. The excitation is **computed at each note by a dynamic hammer–string contact** in the time domain, rather than taken from velocity-indexed tables. The resonators are force-driven recursive filters, not an additive sum of sinusoids with precomputed amplitudes. Finally, no “presynthesis” metamodel is used. On the other hand, two tempting shortcuts come very close to it and deserve examination by a patent attorney before any release earlier than the end of 2027: precomputing the coupled string–soundboard modes by finite elements and then interpolating them with a metamodel as a function of the user sliders, and storing excitation spectra per velocity. Bank et al. (2010) is among the patent's “similar documents”, and Bank's work on the modal piano dates back to 2000–2002 ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). This documents prior art, without prejudging the scope of the claims. Other, more recent patents occupy the field of resonance and dampers: Yamaha US8530736B2 and JP6819309B2, Casio JP6801443B2, JP6540681B2 and JP7230870B2 (Google Patents). The notes contradict each other on US 8,115,092, attributed to “the Kawai family” by one source and presented as a Yamaha patent by the other ([US 8115092](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8115092)). This point should be checked during a freedom-to-operate search. As the project will probably not have a releasable product before 2027, the natural R&D schedule lines up with the expiry of the main patent.

## The string fits in a few hundred modes, but its parameters remain poorly measured

### Inharmonicity B: a reliable formula, contradictory values

The law has been stable since Fletcher: f_k ≈ k f0 √(1 + B k²), with B = κ²(ES/T0)(π/L)² ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). Across the whole keyboard, Rigaud, David and Daudet propose a model with 2 parameters for B and 4 for the tuning curve, which can be estimated from a few bass notes ([HAL hal-00856735](https://hal-imt.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00856735)). DDSP-Piano writes it as B(m) = exp(αT m + βT) + exp(αB m + βB), with **αB = −0.0847, βB = −5.82, αT = 0.0926, βT = −13.64**. The treble asymptote is considered common to all pianos ([Renault et al. 2022](https://dafx.de/paper-archive/2022/papers/DAFx20in22_paper_48.pdf)). In the same work, replacing this explicit model with a learned network degrades the listening test results, which perceptually validates the physical parametrisation ([Renault et al. 2022](https://dafx.de/paper-archive/2022/papers/DAFx20in22_paper_48.pdf)). Yet published measurements diverge by a factor of 2 to 50 depending on the source:

| Note | Rigaud/Renault (this report's calculation) | Fletcher + Steinway D dimensions (forum calculation) | Other |
|---|---|---|---|
| A0 (MIDI 21) | 5.1×10⁻⁴ | 2.85×10⁻⁴ (vendor's forum) | Bass “10⁻⁴ to 10⁻³” ([Edinburgh](https://concept.lib.ed.ac.uk/esjs/article/view/9815)) |
| Minimum (MIDI ≈ 44) | 1.4×10⁻⁴ | — | — |
| C4 (MIDI 60) | 3.3×10⁻⁴ | — | — |

| C3–B4 (mean) | 1.5×10⁻⁴ to 8.6×10⁻⁴ | — | **8.63×10⁻³** (Disklavier, learned network; [Simionato et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/signal-processing/articles/10.3389/frsip.2023.1276748/full)) |
| C8 (MIDI 108) | 2.6×10⁻² | — | — |

The two independent calculations agree remarkably well at A4 but diverge by a factor of 1.8 at A0. This is not surprising: the wound-string region depends on each manufacturer's design. Simionato et al.'s value is an order of magnitude above the others, and the “0.0002 to 0.4” range they cite looks wrong at its upper bound. It should be discarded until it is reproduced. A research note places the minimum of Rigaud's curve “around 3×10⁻⁴ in the middle”. Evaluating the formula, this report instead finds **1.4×10⁻⁴ around MIDI 44**, and 3.3×10⁻⁴ at middle C. Conklin's measured tables (JASA 1996) were not retrieved, and **there is no downloadable public table of measured B across the 88 notes**. For estimation, the fast algorithm by Rauhala, Lehtonen and Välimäki is a proven basis ([JASA 2007](https://www.academia.edu/22085383/Fast_automatic_inharmonicity_estimation_algorithm)).

### Frequency-dependent losses are set per mode

In the modal model, R_k = 1/τ_k = b1 + b3(2πf_k)², or any τ_k value obtained from analysis ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). In the PDE formulation (Bensa/Bilbao), η0 and η1 are used, computed from two T60 values. Only η0 can be modulated in real time without affecting the stability condition ([Risse et al. 2025](https://dafx25.dii.univpm.it/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/DAFx25_paper_24.pdf)). With a DWG, dispersion filters must also be designed: cascades of first-order allpass filters tunable in closed form (Rauhala–Välimäki 2006), robust very-high-order design (Abel–Smith 2006), or improved pole placement (Abel–Välimäki–Smith 2010) ([JOS PASP](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/Dispersion_Filter_Design.html); [CCRMA wiki](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/wiki/Dispersion_Filter_Design)). This design cost disappears with modal synthesis, where B enters the frequencies directly. This is a decisive argument as soon as the user must be able to change the “string length” or the tuning live, as in the commercial modelled piano.

### Phantom partials cost a quarter of the budget, and their origin is debated

At high amplitude, the modulated tension excites the longitudinal modes. These are free modes around 16 to 20 times f0 and forced “phantom” partials at the sums and differences of the transverse frequencies. They are “the free and the forced response of the same system”. The effect matters mostly in the bass, where it “contributes greatly to the metallic character” ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). Bank's real-time algorithm proceeds in four steps. It computes a uniform tension from Σ(k·y_k)², which is already available for the bridge force. It dynamically corrects fewer than 10 longitudinal resonators. It uses a single force for the odd modes and one for the even modes, obtained by correlating the modal vector with a shifted version of itself. It avoids aliasing by ignoring products above fs/2. The whole accounts for **25% of the total cost** of the 2010 piano ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). Bank and Sujbert conclude that transverse→longitudinal coupling is the main source of phantoms ([JASA 2005](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/jasa-longitud/index.html)). Moore, Neldner and Rokni (ASA 2018) claim on the contrary that “only a small part” of their power comes from the string, the rest coming from the bridge and soundboard. However, only a popular-science summary without quantitative data is available ([acoustics.org](https://acoustics.org/tag/piano/)). This contradiction is unresolved. It weighs on the architecture: if the soundboard itself generates phantoms, a linear soundboard model will not reproduce them, which is in tension with the linearity measured at −40 dB (next section). Bank's one-way scheme does not model the tension-induced **pitch glide** either. Modulating it from the already computed T(t) is a simple extension, but one never tested in the sources [inference].

### Double decay requires coupling, not just detuning

Adding detuned strings produces beats, **not** true two-stage decay. That requires a common moving bridge (Weinreich) or an equivalent mechanism ([JOS PASP, Coupled Strings](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/Coupled_Strings.html)). Bank (2010) chooses **banks of secondary resonators**, excited by the hammer force for the beats and double decay, and by the summed outputs of the main strings, through an 8×8 gain matrix per keyboard region (64 MAC), for sympathetic resonance. This one-way coupling is **structurally stable whatever the gains**, but it prevents these banks from being switched off by polyphony management ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). Passive bidirectional (port-Hamiltonian) coupling is physically correct, but Risse et al. mention it without implementing it ([DAFx25](https://dafx25.dii.univpm.it/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/DAFx25_paper_24.pdf)), and **no study measures the perceptual difference between one-way and bidirectional coupling**. Several pieces of data are missing from public sources: measured unison detuning values in cents, frequency differences between polarisations, duplex and aliquot models, agraffe or capo d'astro terminations, and the effective parameters of wound strings.

## The hammer has been discretised without iteration since 2021, but its exponent remains disputed

### The felt law: five formulas per key, an uncertain exponent

Stulov's hereditary model is written F = F0[u^p − (ε/τ)∫u^p(ξ)e^((ξ−t)/τ)dξ]. Its slow limit is Q0·u^p. Its “simple” form, F = Q0[u^p + α·d(u^p)/dt], is a “good approximation in normal playing” ([Stulov & Kartofelev 2009](https://www.tud.ttu.ee/web/dmitri.kartofelev/publications/34IAC2009.pdf); [Smith, PASP](https://www.dsprelated.com/freebooks/pasp/Piano_Hammer_Modeling.html)). Stulov fits per-key laws for n = 1..88: **m = 11.074 − 0.074n + 10⁻⁴n² [g], p = 3.7 + 0.015n, Q0 = 183·e^(0.045n) [N/mm^p]** and α = 248 + 1.83n − 0.055n² [µs]. The signs of the ε(n) and τ(n) laws were lost during extraction and must be checked in Stulov (Acta Acustica, 2005) ([Stulov & Kartofelev 2009](https://www.tud.ttu.ee/web/dmitri.kartofelev/publications/34IAC2009.pdf)). These masses agree well with Chabassier and Duruflé's measurements: the law gives 10.6 g for D#1 against **10.76 g measured**, and 7.4 g for C#5 against 7.90 g ([Chabassier & Duruflé 2014](https://www.math.u-bordeaux.fr/~durufle/article/HammerJSVV2.pdf)) [this report's calculation]. The exponent p, however, is not agreed upon:

| Source | Bass p | Treble p | Nature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stulov's fit | 3.7 | ≈ 5.0 | Fitted formula ([Stulov 2009](https://www.tud.ttu.ee/web/dmitri.kartofelev/publications/34IAC2009.pdf)) |
| Russell, static tests (worn hammers) | 2.2–3.5, no trend | 2.2–3.5 | Measurement ([Russell 1997](https://www.acs.psu.edu/drussell/piano/nonlinearhammer.html)) |
| Russell, static tests (new hammers) | 1.5–2.8 | 1.5–2.8 | Measurement ([Russell 1997](https://www.acs.psu.edu/drussell/piano/nonlinearhammer.html)) |
| Russell, dynamic tests (voiced Steinway) | ≈ 2 (D2: 2.2) | ≈ 4 | Measurement ([Russell 1997](https://www.acs.psu.edu/drussell/piano/nonlinearhammer.html)) |
| Ducceschi et al., test case | 1.3 (α) | — | Numerical demonstration value ([JASA 2021](https://vbn.aau.dk/ws/files/475165562/DucceschiEtal2021JASALinearlyImplicitSchemes.pdf)) |

Russell adds that above p = 3, ff becomes harsh and pp dull, and that the preferred range is 2 < p < 3. Hammers judged “too hard” had a *lower* p than voiced hammers ([Russell 1997](https://www.acs.psu.edu/drussell/piano/nonlinearhammer.html)). The engineering conclusion is clear: **p and Q0 are voicing parameters exposed to the user, not constants**. Numerical conditioning must also be taken into account. In SI units, K = Q0·1000^p reaches 10¹⁵ to 10¹⁸ for p = 4–5. One must therefore work in millimetres or non-dimensionalise [inference]. The closed form of the contact time on a rigid surface, t0 = (2√π·u_max/V)·Γ(1+a)/Γ(b), gives about 0.6 ms for middle C at 3 m/s and about 1.7 ms for key 1 at 1 m/s. This is consistent with Stulov's figure (≈2 ms) and provides an analytical **regression test** ([Stulov & Kartofelev 2009](https://www.tud.ttu.ee/web/dmitri.kartofelev/publications/34IAC2009.pdf)). On a real string, which moves, the contact lasts longer.

### The numerical scheme: explicit quadratisation, at fixed cost

Ducceschi, Bilbao, Willemsen and Serafin write the collision potential as φ = ψ²/2, with ψ as an independent state at half steps. The update becomes **explicit (“solved by simple division”)**, the discrete energy is conserved to machine precision, and stability only requires k < 2√(M/K), whatever the nonlinearity. A safeguard (g = 0 if g·η ≥ 4ψ) guarantees that the force is always repulsive. Iterative schemes, by contrast, see their iteration count grow with stiffness, which the authors consider “important with a view to a real-time implementation”. Accuracy drops to first order when Kη/(Mk) > 1. With very stiff hammers, the discrepancies seen at 44.1 kHz disappear at 5×44.1 kHz ([Ducceschi et al. 2021](https://vbn.aau.dk/ws/files/475165562/DucceschiEtal2021JASALinearlyImplicitSchemes.pdf)). Van Walstijn et al. transpose this recipe to a modal basis. OF = 1 is enough for the contact at 44.1 kHz, OF = 2 is “nominally correct” under varying tension, and parameter updates in blocks of 128 leave artefacts more than 60 dB below the partials ([DAFx23](https://www.dafx.de/paper-archive/2023/DAFx23_paper_18.pdf)). Stulov's hereditary term adds only one state, since the exponential kernel can be computed recursively by a one-pole filter on u^p [inference]. The contact lasts only 0.5 to 4 ms, so local ×4 to ×8 oversampling during that interval alone remains affordable [inference].

### From MIDI velocity to touch: a measurable mapping

On reproducing pianos, hammer velocity follows a roughly logarithmic curve of MIDI velocity ([MMD 2003](https://www.mmdigest.com/Archives/Digests/200308/2003.08.25.01.html)). Stulov takes V ≤ 5 m/s as the normal range ([Stulov 2009](https://www.tud.ttu.ee/web/dmitri.kartofelev/publications/34IAC2009.pdf)). The figure of about 8 m/s for a struck touch comes only from a secondary summary of Goebl, Bresin and Galembo ([summary](https://composition.gregspero.com/lessons/a2/)). Fioretti et al. (DAFx 2025) show on an instrumented concert action that the best predictor of impact velocity is the key velocity just before escapement. The classic “averaged A→C” predictor is worse at the extremes, and their dataset is public ([DAFx25 paper 38](https://dafx25.dii.univpm.it/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/DAFx25_paper_38.pdf)). The commercial modelled piano's manual states that a measured acoustic piano offers about **50 dB of dynamic range**, a useful target for calibrating velocity→loudness (vendor's manual, v4). The 2025 MIDI-CI Piano Profile standardises a default velocity curve (pppp–ffff) in MIDI 1.0 and 2.0, but its values were not retrieved ([midi.org](https://midi.org/midi-association-and-amei-release-the-piano-profile-and-implementation-guide)).

Touch beyond velocity involves two mechanisms. The first is **offline**. Chabassier and Duruflé show that with a flexible shank, two notes of equal loudness played legato and staccato differ by up to **20 dB between 500 and 1000 Hz**, and that the let-off distance (1.5 versus 3 mm) also changes the spectrum ([Chabassier & Duruflé 2014](https://www.math.u-bordeaux.fr/~durufle/article/HammerJSVV2.pdf)). The second is perceptually established. Goebl, Bresin and Fujinaga show that listeners can tell apart two touches at identical hammer velocity **only if the finger–key noises are present** ([Goebl et al. 2014](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Werner-Goebl/publication/269274469_GoeblBresinFujinaga2014-JASA-PianoTouchQuality1/links/548576440cf24356db60f1e0/GoeblBresinFujinaga2014-JASA-PianoTouchQuality1.pdf)). The two results do not contradict each other. The simulation exhibits a structural mechanism that the listening experiment did not isolate, but for a MIDI input without a continuous position sensor, the noises carry most of the effect. Multibody action models (Hirschkorn: 5 bodies and 13 contacts; McPhee: flexible shank) remain offline tools ([Hirschkorn, UWSpace](https://uwspace.uwaterloo.ca/handle/10012/877); [Izadbakhsh](https://uwspace.uwaterloo.ca/handle/10012/3110)).

### Dampers and pedals: no measured physical law

There is **no measured damper-felt contact law in the reviewed literature**. The patents of the US 8,115,092 / 8,895,831 family vary a viscous coefficient b_D over time, and an intermediate value naturally represents half-damping ([US 8115092](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8115092); [US 8895831](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8895831)). Bank (2010) merely shortens the decay times of the resonators ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). The commercial modelled piano exposes a “damper position” that determines which harmonics are less well damped (vendor's manual, v4), and a forum post describes pedal release as wool–steel Coulomb friction, F = μ·F_N (vendor's forum). In practice, CC64 is used continuously, but some pedals cover only part of the range (Yamaha FC3: 0–68) and many MIDI files contain only 0/64/127 (vendor's forum). There is **neither a measured damper-height/CC64 curve, nor una corda displacement values, nor a minimum repetition lift**.

### Mechanical noises are mimetic everywhere

The literature and the products handle action noises with event-triggered impulses filtered by cabinet resonances. Patent US 5,286,915 synthesises the impact of the key against its stop from the negative part of a filtered accelerometer signal ([US 5286915](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5286915)). The 2004 reference harpsichord even triggers a sample ([EURASIP 2004](https://doaj.org/article/8724dc0c97f64d13ac03db29cfccb353)). Chabassier's model explicitly excludes impacts on the structure ([Chabassier & Duruflé 2014](https://www.math.u-bordeaux.fr/~durufle/article/HammerJSVV2.pdf)). The commercial modelled piano separately exposes hammer noise, release noise (12 dB of variation with note-off velocity for the Blüthner) and pedal noise (vendor's forum). **No source provides a physical model or measured spectra of the key-bottom impact, the release, the damper return or the pedal.** The best sample-free option is a contact impulse (Hertz or Hunt–Crossley) exciting a small modal bank representing the structure. This is a “physically inspired” approach, but its parameters will have to be tuned by ear or measured with an impact hammer [inference].

## The soundboard is linear to −40 dB: a shared modal bank is enough

### Well-characterised physics, data only as curves

Ege, Boutillon and Rébillat measured, on a piano being played, a **soundboard nonlinearity about 40 dB below the linear part at ff**, and modal damping close to that of wood ([arXiv 1212.2323](https://arxiv.org/abs/1212.2323)). The soundboard behaves as a clamped homogeneous plate up to about 1.1 kHz, then as a few waveguides bounded by the ribs. On an upright, they measure M = 9 kg, a modal density of 1/19.5 mode/Hz, a loss factor η ≈ 2% and a mean mobility of about **1.3×10⁻³ s/kg (≈ 800 kg/s) between 100 and 1000 Hz**, with fluctuations of ±10 to 15 dB and a drop to 230 kg/s around 2.5 kHz ([Ege & Boutillon, ar5iv 1210.5688](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1210.5688)). Their “synthetic mobility” formulas make it possible to generate a statistically correct admittance from a few parameters. The mean is G_C = n(f)/(4M), and the resonance and antiresonance envelopes are G_C·β and G_C/β, with β = 2/(πμ) and μ = n·η·f ([ar5iv 1210.5688](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1210.5688)). **Only four sets of bridge mobility data are published** (Wogram, Nakamura, Conklin, Giordano), all in the normal direction and all as curves. Conklin's, considered the most reliable, show that assembling the strings and frame lowers the peaks by about 15 dB. Nothing is published above 3.2 kHz ([ar5iv 1210.5688](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1210.5688)). The figures also diverge: Giordano gives 1 to 2×10³ kg/s near a rib against 0.6 to 0.7×10³ kg/s between ribs, and Weinreich considers Wogram's high-frequency drop an artefact. Prestress (crown, string downbearing) shifts the eigenfrequencies and requires a nonlinear prestressed finite-element computation ([HAL hal-01674967](https://hal.sorbonne-universite.fr/hal-01674967)).

### What runs in real time: convolution or a fixed-pole parallel filter

Bank (2010) couples strings and soundboard in one direction only, justifying this choice by a soundboard impedance “about a thousand times” that of the string. Impedance effects (shift and damping of partials) are built into each string's parameters. He compares two options. The first is a partitioned convolution with a measured force→pressure response: 1000 to 2000 points are enough for the tone, but the “knock” of middle and treble notes requires “tens of thousands” of points, i.e. 633 flops/sample for 20,000 points with N = 128. The second is a **fixed-pole parallel filter**: 100 second-order sections with log-spaced poles (R = 0.98), numerators solved by least squares, stereo outputs sharing the poles, and each section interpretable as a soundboard mode ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). He also notes that the response varies with the excitation point on the bridge [RT proven]. For the “zero samples” constraint, this distinction matters. **A convolution with a measured response is a sample** and falls outside the specification. A parallel filter *fitted* to a measurement is a compressed parametric representation of a measurement, hence a mimetic object. Only a soundboard whose poles and residues come from a model (Ege–Boutillon statistics or finite-element modal analysis) is physical in the strict sense. Bank himself considered computing the soundboard response offline by finite differences or finite elements “at calibration rate” ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). Chabassier provides the order of magnitude: **2400 modes cover 0–10 kHz** for a Steinway D, with a damping law f_d(λ) = αλ + β√λ + γ ([M2AN Part 2](https://www.numdam.org/item/10.1051/m2an/2015007.pdf)). At about 4 MAC per mode, that makes roughly 10,000 MAC per sample [inference], which is very little.

### Sympathetic resonance: from the reverberant register to the 1500-section bank

Zambon, Lehtonen and Bank model the sustain pedal with a feed-forward block of **1500 second-order sections (≈ 6000 MAC/sample)**. The block is calibrated by FZ-ARMA on impulse responses of a Steinway C measured with the dampers down and then raised ([DAFx 2008](https://dafx.de/paper-archive/2008/papers/dafx08_37.pdf)). Older models made do with 12 to 28 simplified strings ([DAFx 2008](https://dafx.de/paper-archive/2008/papers/dafx08_37.pdf)). A physical bank of all the strings, about 230 strings with 40 modes each, would cost on the order of 28,000 MAC/sample, which is affordable in 2026 and remains stable with one-way coupling [inference]. The pedal lengthens the decays and adds a noise floor to all notes ([Schutz et al., AES 2008](https://eurecom.fr/publication/2478/download/cm-schuan-080517.pdf)).

### Radiation and microphones: residues per microphone

According to the vendor's forum, the commercial modelled piano simulates a soundboard “roughly shaped like the case” and computes the pressure at the microphone point, the microphone being an ideal omnidirectional (vendor's forum). This is a research excerpt that has not been verified directly. Directivity data exist but are not downloadable. Nakashima, Giron and Tohyama measured a 2 m sphere in 10° steps and derived minimum-phase filters explicitly intended for synthesis ([ASA 1996](https://auditory.org/asamtgs/asa96ind/3aAA/3aAA10.html)). Plath, Bader et al. recorded 1289 near-field positions on two concert soundboards, with 15 excitation points on the bridge ([ISMA 2017](https://isma2017.cirmmt.mcgill.ca/proceedings/pdf/ISMA_2017_paper_16.pdf)). Since the soundboard modal bank shares its poles, **each microphone is just an additional set of residues**, as Bank notes for stereo ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). These residues can be precomputed with a Rayleigh integral over each mode shape, with an image source for the lid. An alternative is to use 16 to 64 equivalent sources distributed over the soundboard, each with a delay and a 1/r attenuation towards each microphone [inference]. The room remains a separate stage. A convolution with a measured room response would also be a sample, so an FDN or a synthetic response is needed [inference].

### What does not exist and will have to be produced or estimated

The list of data absent from the public domain is long and dictates part of the plan. There is no numerical table of measured B across 88 notes (Conklin 1996 exists only as figures). There is no numerical dataset of bridge admittance by position (Conklin, Giordano, Wogram and Nakamura are curves, and the raw Hamburg data are not online). Also missing: unison detuning values and the difference between polarisations, the strike point per note (empirical rule of about 1/7 to 1/9, unsourced), any damper-felt law and the half-pedal curve, the una corda displacement and the struck felt area, any modal data on the keyboard, key rail or pedal mechanism for noises, and any duplex or aliquot data. The exact signs of ε(n) and τ(n) in Stulov remain to be confirmed. Chaigne and Askenfelt's tables (b1, b3, ε for C2, C4 and C7) were not retrieved. Three routes exist to fill these gaps: digitising published figures, estimating from samples (analysis only), or measuring oneself with an impact hammer on the bridge and the keyboard, microphones at known positions and recordings with the dampers raised and lowered. A last pitfall concerns identifiability. On a mono recording, cabinet equalisation, hammer spectrum, strike point and room response become confounded ([VladPetk/nn_physical_model_piano](https://github.com/VladPetk/nn_physical_model_piano)), hence the value of multi-microphone recordings at known positions.

Steinberg released the VST 3.8 SDK under the **MIT licence** in October 2025 ([Sonicstate](https://sonicstate.com/news/2025/10/30/vst-3-now-available-under-mit-license)). The GPLv3 lock-in therefore only comes from the Rust bindings used by nih-plug and nice-plug, and the issue requesting permissive bindings remains open ([nih-plug issues](https://github.com/robbert-vdh/nih-plug/issues)). The implications are simple. A GPLv3 project can ship CLAP, VST3 and the standalone application without difficulty. A permissive or closed-source project must stick to CLAP and the standalone application, or wait for, or write, permissive VST3 bindings. The ASIO SDK, for its part, moved to GPLv3 with a dual-licensing option, which weighs on a Windows standalone application ([Sonicstate](https://sonicstate.com/news/2025/10/30/vst-3-now-available-under-mit-license)). No code should be taken from OpenPiano (AGPL-3.0) ([GitHub](https://github.com/michele-perrone/OpenPiano)). faust-coopiano (MIT) can serve as a reference ([GitHub](https://github.com/sletz/faust-coopiano)). Risse et al.'s C++ code is public, but its licence is not given in the notes.

On the compute side, Anukari's experience settles the GPU question. This physical synth **moved from GPU to CPU in November 2025**, with hand-written AVX2/NEON SIMD and one thread per voice, and became “significantly faster” in almost all cases. The GPU required buffers of at least ~512 samples, and automatic vectorisation proved “very conservative” ([Anukari part 1](https://anukari.com/blog/devlog/anukari-on-the-cpu-part-1-gpu-issues); [part 2](https://anukari.com/blog/devlog/anukari-on-the-cpu-part-2-cpu-optimization); [KVR](https://www.kvraudio.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=9078822)). A back-of-the-envelope calculation puts a modern AVX2 core at between **2×10⁴ and 10⁵ damped modes at 48 kHz**, depending on the efficiency achieved. A piano voice has 200 to 600 modes, i.e. 50 to 200 voices per core [inference, not measured]. This is the central hypothesis to verify first.

## Architecture decision: modal core and quadratised contact, SAV finite differences as a research track

| Criterion | **Bank-style modal + SAV/IEQ hammer** (recommended) | Waveguide (DWG) | Explicit SAV FD (Risse 2025) | Energy-based implicit FD/FE (Chabassier) | Metamodel + additive (as in the vendor's patent) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Complete piano demonstrated in real time | **Yes** (2010, with SSE) | Yes (Borin, Bank, Rauhala) | No: strings only | No (~10⁷ times too slow) | Yes (the commercial modelled piano) |
| Cost per string | ~3 MAC × 100–300 modes | The lowest | 1–3.6% of an M3 core | Out of reach | Low online, heavy offline |
| Inharmonicity editable live | Direct (per-mode frequencies) | Dispersion filters must be redesigned | Via β, subject to the CFL condition | — | Via the metamodel |
| Phantoms and longitudinal nonlinearity | Yes, Bank's algorithm (25% of the cost) | Not very physical (non-orthogonal modes) | Transverse cubic; no longitudinal | Exact | Depends on the data |
| Hammer contact | Explicit quadratised, stable, fixed cost | WDF port, multirate, or unstable unit delay | To be integrated, not published | Iterative implicit | Excitation stored per velocity |
| Re-striking a sounding string | Natural (state preserved) | Yes (not commuted) | Natural | Natural | Approximated |
| Stability guarantee | Quadratised energy; one-way coupling structurally stable | Relies on filter design | Energy, plus drift control | Energy | LTI |
| Bidirectional coupling at the bridge | Possible via modal admittance, not demonstrated | Easy (common termination) | Port-Hamiltonian considered, not implemented | Yes | Solved offline |
| Proximity to the vendor's patent | Moderate (modal synthesis, but dynamic excitation) | Low | Low | Low | **High** |

The decision is a **modal core of the Bank, Zambon and Fontana type**, with explicit energy-quadratised hammer contact (van Walstijn 2023 and Ducceschi 2021), the longitudinal nonlinearity of Bank 2010 in the bass, detuned secondary banks for unison and sympathy with one-way coupling, and a shared modal soundboard whose microphones are sets of residues. Four reasons support it. It is the only architecture that has demonstrated the whole specification in real time. It makes all the commercial piano's parameters (stretching, string length, per-partial spectral profile, damper position) natural, since they act mode by mode. It vectorises trivially, each resonator being an independent recurrence. And it keeps the excitation dynamic, which sets it apart from the patent. The waveguide is excluded from the core, because its limitation on nonlinearity is structural and its dispersion filters complicate live editing. SAV finite differences remain a research track (Phase 2b), to be reopened if a publication shows a complete coupled piano or if listening tests reveal a shortcoming of the modal approach regarding pitch glide and geometric nonlinearity. At 1–4% of a core per string, 88 × 3 strings would require 3 to 9 cores even before the hammer and coupling [inference]. Two findings would force the decision to be revisited: a Phase 0 microbenchmark that invalidated the estimate of 10⁴ to 10⁵ modes per core, or a quantitative confirmation that phantoms arise mainly in the structure (Moore 2018), which would require a nonlinearity at the bridge that is absent from all current real-time models.

### Phase 0: a test bench that settles the CPU question before anything else

**Objective.** Make every later decision measurable. **Technique.** The DSP engine lives in a crate independent of any framework (`process(&mut self, block)`), with a deterministic offline MIDI→WAV rendering binary, following Anukari's practice of reference tests by FFT comparison ([devlog](https://anukari.com/blog/devlog)). The analysis includes partial tracking, B estimation (algorithm by [Rauhala et al. 2007](https://www.academia.edu/22085383/Fast_automatic_inharmonicity_estimation_algorithm)), per-partial T60 and two-slope fitting, high-resolution pole estimation (FZ-ARMA [Zambon 2008](https://dafx.de/paper-archive/2008/papers/dafx08_37.pdf) or ESPRIT), attack spectral centroid, multi-resolution STFT distances and FAD with a declared embedding ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/frechet-audio-distance/); scores are not comparable from one embedding to another according to [arXiv 2609.18216](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.18216)). pyo3 bindings expose the Rust kernels to scipy for fitting. The CI runs assert_no_alloc and rtsan-standalone. **Decisive experiment**: a criterion microbenchmark of an SoA resonator bank in f32, pulp kernel versus scalar version, with and without input, to measure modes per core and per fs. It validates or invalidates the 2×10⁴ to 10⁵ modes hypothesis. **Data.** Ingestion of Salamander and Iowa, digitisation of Conklin's and Giordano's curves, and a measurement plan if an instrument is available (impact hammer on the bridge, recordings with the dampers raised and lowered, microphones at known positions). **Risks.** Underestimating the analysis tooling, which is the real bottleneck; denormals in decay tails, to be handled by FTZ/DAZ at each callback. **Criteria.** On synthetic signals with known ground truth, the extractors recover B, T60 and the double-decay parameters to within a few per cent (tolerance to be set, unsourced project target). Rendering is bit-identical from one run to the next. Throughput is documented on at least one x86 AVX2 machine and one ARM machine. **Open questions.** Is f32 sufficient, without frequency drift, for very lightly damped bass modes? Are f64 poles needed for A0?

### Phase 1: a string and a hammer that never diverge

**Objective.** One voice per note, physically complete on the excitation side, stable across the whole keyboard. **Technique.** The modal string follows Bank's equation (1), with frequencies taken from Rigaud's B(m) by default, losses b1 + b3ω² or fitted τ_k, and impulse-invariance discretisation ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). The hammer follows the Q0·u^p law with hysteresis (simple or recursive hereditary form) and Stulov's initial values; p and Q0 are exposed ([Stulov 2009](https://www.tud.ttu.ee/web/dmitri.kartofelev/publications/34IAC2009.pdf)). The contact is integrated with the explicit quadratised scheme in a modal basis, with removal of the modes above 0.9·Nyquist ([van Walstijn 2023](https://www.dafx.de/paper-archive/2023/DAFx23_paper_18.pdf); [Ducceschi 2021](https://vbn.aau.dk/ws/files/475165562/DucceschiEtal2021JASALinearlyImplicitSchemes.pdf)), in non-dimensionalised variables or in millimetres. The bass receives the longitudinal modes via even/odd correlation (K < 10) ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). Hammer velocity follows V = V_min·(V_max/V_min)^((vel−1)/126), with V_max between 5 and 7 m/s as a parameter [inference], and an f32 input in [0,1] ready for MIDI 2.0. **Nature.** Physical. **Risks.** The conditioning of K; the loss of accuracy to first order when Kη/(Mk) > 1 in the hard treble, to be compensated by local oversampling during the contact; audible colouring from aliasing of the longitudinal products. **CPU.** A0 has about 300 modes, i.e. roughly 1% of a core under the low hypothesis; the longitudinal block (≈ 1000 MAC/sample naive, less with the even/odd trick) represents a comparable cost; the hammer adds O(M) for only 1 to 4 ms [inference]. **Criteria.** The discrete Hamiltonian never grows without input (debug invariant) and a sweep of 88 notes × 127 velocities × rapid re-strikes produces no divergence. The contact time decreases with V and stays above the analytical rigid-surface bound. Partial frequencies match B to within one cent, which verifies the discretisation. The attack centroid is monotonic in velocity and comparable to Salamander's 16 layers. The dynamic range reaches about 50 dB (vendor's manual, v4). For listening, blind note-by-note A/B comparisons against Salamander. **Open experiments.** Russell's p (2–4) versus Stulov's (3.7–5) by ear; the audible contribution of the pitch glide obtained by modulating the frequencies with T(t); OF = 1 versus OF = 2 during the contact.

### Phase 2: unison and bridge, the leap to real polyphony

**Objective.** Credible beats and double decay, then full real-time polyphony. **Technique.** One main string and one or two detuned secondary strings per note, excited by the hammer force, with distinct decays, in the manner of Bank (2010). One-way coupling is stable by construction ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). The unison width parameter follows that of the commercial modelled piano. Voices are managed by cost budget rather than by note count ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). The SIMD kernel is parallelised per voice, with single-threaded SIMD by default in a plugin and a pool of real-time-promoted workers in the standalone application ([audio_thread_priority](https://github.com/mozilla/audio_thread_priority)). Parameters change at calibration rate (20 to 50 Hz) and are swapped lock-free. **Research track 2b.** (a) Passive bidirectional coupling via the bridge modal admittance Y_b(ω) = Σ residues/poles, following Weinreich, to obtain double decay and sympathetic transfer “for free”. (b) An explicit SAV FD string with drift control, in an A/B comparison ([Risse 2025](https://dafx25.dii.univpm.it/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/DAFx25_paper_24.pdf)). **Nature.** Physical, with a deliberately simplified coupling. **Risks.** One-way coupling must “bake” the impedance effects into each string, which multiplies the parameters to calibrate, especially since measured detuning values are not publicly available. Threads internal to a plugin may conflict with the host's pool. **CPU.** With about 20,000 to 35,000 active modes with the pedal down, **0.3 to 1.5 cores** are needed, depending on the throughput measured in Phase 0 [inference]. Bank 2010 splits the cost into 40% transverse, 30% secondary, 25% longitudinal and 5% hammer ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). **Criteria.** Decay slopes and beat rates within the range of those measured on Salamander and Iowa, note by note. Zero xruns over 10 minutes of MAESTRO at 128 samples and 48 kHz, with a margin of at least 30% of the period. Stability for any coupling gain (randomised test). **Open questions.** Is bidirectional coupling audibly different from one-way coupling? No source answers this, hence a dedicated MUSHRA test. Is a second polarisation necessary?

### Phase 3: dampers, pedals and sympathetic resonance, where physics beats sampling

**Objective.** The behaviours that sampling renders poorly and that the mass market already models: half-pedal, sympathy, partial damping. **Technique.** The damper is a viscous force −R_d(t)·v(x_d) applied at point x_d of each string and projected onto the modes. Its position produces a sin(nπx_d/L) comb of less-damped partials, which reproduces the commercial modelled piano's “damper position” [inference], and a soft unilateral stiffness is optional. The damper height h(t) depends on the key, the note-off velocity and continuous CC64, with slew-rate limiting. Sostenuto is a logical latch; una corda is a selection of 2 strings out of 3 with a different felt zone (Q0 and p). For sympathy, two options are to be compared: Bank's 8×8 region matrix, or a bank of all strings (~230 strings at ~40 modes, ≈ 28,000 MAC/sample) fed by the bridge bus ([Bank et al. 2010](https://home.mit.bme.hu/~bank/publist/taslp10.pdf)). Duplex and aliquot are undamped segments tuned to harmonics and fed by the same bus [inference, no source]. **Nature.** Semi-physical: the structure is physical, but **no damper contact law has been measured**, and both the patents and Bank use a viscous coefficient ([US 8115092](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8115092)). **Risks.** “Zipper” noise on the damping coefficients; implausible pedal behaviour for lack of a measured curve; MIDI pedals with a truncated range, hence a pedal calibration step. **CPU.** +0.3 to 0.6 core with the pedal down in the “all strings” option [inference]. **Criteria.** MAESTRO renders with continuous pedalling free of artefacts; a pedal up/down difference whose decay lengthening and noise floor are comparable to published observations ([Schutz 2008](https://eurecom.fr/publication/2478/download/cm-schuan-080517.pdf)) and to our own measurements if they exist; a continuous half-pedal transition. **Open experiments.** Measure, if possible, the extinction time of each partial as a function of damper pressure; should damping be frequency-selective (treble damped first)? Should the release noise use a Coulomb friction model, as described on the vendor's forum?

### Phase 4: soundboard and microphones, from statistics to finite elements

**Objective.** Replace any measured response with a computed soundboard and make microphone placement physical. **Technique.** The soundboard is a shared modal bank (Bank's parallel form) excited by the bridge force, summed per bridge region. Its poles come, in order of priority, (a) from the Ege–Boutillon statistics (n(f) ≈ 0.05 mode/Hz below 1 kHz, η ≈ 2%, mean mobility ≈ 800 to 2000 kg/s) ([ar5iv 1210.5688](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1210.5688)), then (b) from an offline modal analysis of a ribbed orthotropic plate, in the spirit of Chabassier (up to 2400 modes at 10 kHz) ([M2AN](https://www.numdam.org/item/10.1051/m2an/2015007.pdf)). Fitting to measurements (FZ-ARMA then least squares) serves only to **calibrate and validate**. Each microphone receives residues precomputed with a Rayleigh integral, with an image source for the lid. The room is algorithmic (FDN), which rules out any measured impulse response. **Nature.** Physical (finite elements) or physically grounded (statistical). A per-microphone equalisation fitted to directivity data would be mimetic and must be labelled as such. **Risks.** The “knock” of middle and treble notes requires a long response, i.e. tens of thousands of points as an FIR according to Bank, and therefore enough well-placed soundboard modes. The regime above 1.1 kHz, localised between the ribs, is poorly described by a homogeneous plate ([arXiv 1305.3057](https://arxiv.org/abs/1305.3057v1)). Prestress (crown, downbearing) requires nonlinear finite elements. The mobility data stop at 3.2 kHz. **CPU.** 2400 modes × ~4 MAC plus 2 MAC per mode per microphone, i.e. about 30,000 MAC/sample for 8 microphones: a few per cent of a core [inference]. **Criteria.** A simulated bridge-point mobility within the ranges of Conklin, Giordano and Ege–Boutillon. A plausible spectral variation when the microphone moves. A “knock” that is present in comparative listening. STFT distances and FAD on MAESTRO pieces, with a render of the commercial modelled piano as the reference. **Open questions.** Should the bridge–soundboard loop be closed in both directions (related to Phase 2b)? How many soundboard modes are perceptually necessary? Are the Hamburg data (Plath, Bader) available on request?

### Phase 5: noises, calibration and user parameters, where mimicry must be accepted

**Objective.** Functional parity with the commercial modelled piano and reproducible calibration. **Technique.** For noises, a Hunt–Crossley contact impulse (duration and amplitude derived from velocity) excites small modal banks: key bottom and frame at note-on (earlier and louder for a struck touch), key rail and backcheck at release (note-off velocity), pedal mechanism (derivative of CC64) and damper at re-contact. The modes are tuned by ear or measured with an impact hammer. Calibration is done by offline differentiable fitting of B, detuning, τ_k and hammer hardness versus velocity, on Salamander or Iowa, with physical priors and smoothing regularisation against the identifiability problem. DDSP-Piano's explicit B shows that the physical structure must be kept ([Renault 2022](https://dafx.de/paper-archive/2022/papers/DAFx20in22_paper_48.pdf)). The interface follows the commercial piano's list (tuning, voicing, design, action, pedals, microphones, per-note editing with interpolation between control notes), with a MIDI-CI Piano Profile responder. **Nature.** **The noises are mimetic**, plainly. No source provides a physical model or measured spectrum, and the most complete physical model excludes them. They are “physically inspired”, not simulated. **Risks.** Detectably “synthetic” noises. A calibration that compensates for a model defect with an aberrant parameter, to be monitored with physical bounds. Data licences. **CPU.** Negligible. **Criteria.** A MUSHRA test with at least 15 trained listeners, a 1 kHz low-pass anchor and a hidden reference (recording), including a “commercial piano render” condition, analysed with Friedman and post-hoc tests, as in [Simionato et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.06513). A FAD with the declared embedding. A touch discrimination test at equal hammer velocity, following Goebl, with and without noises. **Open questions.** What real perceptual gain do noises bring compared with their absence? Can one measure a keyboard oneself to partly escape mimicry?

## Conclusion

The main lesson contradicts the initial intuition. The challenge of “a free modelled piano in Rust” is no longer algorithmic. Bank solved the overall real-time architecture in 2010, Ducceschi and van Walstijn solved fixed-cost hammer stability between 2021 and 2023, and a modern CPU probably has an order of magnitude of headroom. The challenge is **epistemic**. Most of the parameters that make a piano sound the way it does (B note by note, bridge admittance, unison detunings, damper laws, keyboard modes) are not available in usable form, and the few published figures diverge by a factor of 2 to 50. The project will therefore gain more from investing early in analysis and measurement than from optimising the kernel. This choice also has an effect on the patent: an engine whose excitation is simulated and whose parameters come from its own measurements moves that much further away from the “metamodel and stored excitations” scheme.

Two areas remain to be explored and could give the project an original contribution rather than a mere reimplementation. The first is passive bidirectional bridge–soundboard coupling in a modal basis, which no one has yet evaluated perceptually in real time. The second is the origin of phantom partials (string or structure), which will decide whether a nonlinearity at the bridge is needed. Mechanical noises are the only part of the specification where the “physical rather than mimetic” requirement cannot be met with the current literature. It is better to state this explicitly than to hide it.
